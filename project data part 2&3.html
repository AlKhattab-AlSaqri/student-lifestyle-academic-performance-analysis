Analysis of Students’ Lifestyle and Academic Performance

Author

Al Khattab Al Saqri 138349

Introduction:

Students’ academic performance is influenced by a combination of behavioral and lifestyle factors, including study habits, sleep patterns, social engagement, and physical activity. In recent years, there has been increasing interest in understanding how these factors interact not only to affect academic outcomes such as Grade Point Average (GPA), but also students’ psychological well-being, often reflected through stress levels.

From a statistical perspective, analyzing such relationships provides an opportunity to apply both inferential and model-based techniques to real-world data. By examining patterns within student lifestyle variables, we can assess whether observed differences and associations are statistically significant, and whether they can be used to build reliable predictive models.

This study is based on a dataset that includes key lifestyle indicators—study hours, sleep hours, social hours, and physical activity—along with academic performance measured by GPA and a categorical variable representing stress level. The combination of continuous and categorical variables allows for a comprehensive analysis using a range of statistical methods.

The primary objective of this report is to apply appropriate inferential procedures and advanced statistical modeling techniques to explore relationships within the data, test relevant hypotheses, and evaluate the ability of lifestyle factors to explain and predict academic performance and stress levels. Emphasis is placed on selecting suitable methods, validating underlying assumptions, and providing meaningful interpretations of the results in the context of student life.

Research Questions

  1. Is there a significant relationship between study hours and academic performance (GPA)?

  2. How do lifestyle factors (sleep hours, social hours, and physical activity) relate to students’ GPA?

  3. Do students with different stress levels (low, medium, high) differ significantly in their lifestyle habits?

  4. Is there a statistically significant association between lifestyle variables and stress level?

  5. Can students’ stress levels be accurately predicted using lifestyle variables?

  6. Are there distinct groups of students based on their lifestyle patterns?

      Interpretation:

      Multiple Imputation using the MICE (Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations) approach is the most appropriate method for handling the missing values in this dataset because it provides statistically sound and unbiased estimates while making full use of the available information.

      First, the missing values in this dataset are relatively low in proportion but are spread across several variables rather than concentrated in a single one. In such situations, deleting incomplete cases would lead to unnecessary loss of information and a reduction in statistical power. This could also distort the representativeness of the sample, especially if the missingness is not completely random.

      Second, MICE is particularly suitable because it accounts for the multivariate relationships among variables. In this dataset, variables such as study hours, sleep hours, social activity, physical activity, and GPA are likely interrelated. MICE preserves these relationships by imputing missing values based on predictive models using the other observed variables, rather than relying on simple replacements such as mean or median.

      Third, this method reduces bias compared to single imputation techniques. Simple approaches like mean imputation ignore variability and tend to underestimate standard errors, which can lead to overly optimistic statistical conclusions. In contrast, MICE creates multiple imputed datasets, reflecting the uncertainty of missing values and producing more reliable standard errors and confidence intervals after pooling results.

      Finally, MICE is flexible and suitable for mixed data types, which is important in this dataset that includes both continuous variables (e.g., GPA, hours-based variables) and categorical variables (Stress_Level).

      Overall, Multiple Imputation (MICE) is the best choice because it preserves data integrity, maintains statistical relationships, reduces bias, and provides more valid inferential results compared to simpler or case-deletion methods.

Conclusion

This study examines the interplay between students’ academic performance, stress levels, and daily lifestyle behaviors, utilizing exploratory analysis, inferential statistics, regression modeling, and clustering techniques. Key findings indicate that study behavior is the primary factor influencing academic performance, with a strong positive correlation between study hours and GPA. In contrast, other factors like sleep, social activity, and physical activity showed weak links to GPA, with physical activity negatively correlating with it.

Stress levels vary significantly across GPA groups, suggesting high-performing students endure more pressure, although this does not imply a benefit. Lifestyle behaviors were found to greatly impact stress; increased study hours correlate with higher stress, while longer sleep duration reduces it. High-stress students typically study more but sleep less and engage less in physical and social activities.

Clustering analysis identified three distinct student lifestyle profiles, highlighting the diversity in students’ time allocation for academic versus non-academic pursuits, which may inform targeted interventions.

  • Final Insight:

    The findings highlight the importance of maintaining a balanced student lifestyle. Although academic success is strongly driven by study effort, excessive focus on studying without adequate sleep, physical activity, and social interaction may contribute to higher stress levels. Therefore, promoting balanced time management strategies is essential for supporting both academic achievement and student well-being.
